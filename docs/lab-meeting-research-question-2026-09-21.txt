====================================================================
1. OPENING SENTENCE - WHY THIS MATTERS
====================================================================

Cities and researchers now rely on thousands of low-cost air-quality sensors to
see pollution where regulatory monitors cannot reach, but the small printed
enclosure that protects each sensor can warm the air it is trying to measure by
several degrees - biasing not only the reported temperature and humidity, but
the pollutant concentrations that are corrected using them.

====================================================================
2. LITERATURE REVIEW (funnel: broad shielding physics -> the printed
   air-quality enclosure)
====================================================================

That a sensor's shield, not the atmosphere, can set the measurement error is
long established: Nakamura and Mahrt (2005) compared a naturally ventilated
multiplate shield against an aspirated reference over a full season and found a
mean daytime error of 0.39 degC that exceeded 0.5 degC in 23 percent of daytime
periods, with the error largest at low wind and high solar radiation and well
described by a single ratio of radiative load to ventilation. Work on low-cost
shielding then asked how far cheap geometry can close that gap: Tarara and
Hoheisel (2007) held sensor and material constant while varying shield geometry
and found overlapping stacked plates gave the smallest daytime error while open
bottoms and weakly perforated tubes produced large warm errors, and Holden et
al. (2013) showed an inexpensive white shield could approach commercial
performance while still retaining a warm bias under full sun and low wind. More
recent work extended this to fabricated and 3D-printed hardware - Theisen et al.
(2020) on long-term printed weather-station durability, Botero-Valencia et al.
(2022) on printed shield material and geometry, and Deford et al. (2025) on
active aspiration, which reduces bias further at the cost of power and
complexity - and together these studies converge on ventilation, surface
reflectance and sensor isolation as the controlling design variables. A parallel
strand tried to model rather than rank: Bernard et al. (2019) derived an
energy-balance model that compresses a shelter's error into two physically
interpretable coefficients, a thermal response time and a radiation sensitivity,
and improved error prediction by roughly 15 percent over earlier empirical
schemes across twelve shelters - but those coefficients are obtained by
regression against a reference after the shelter exists, and the authors state
explicitly that the relationship between the coefficients and the shelter's
physical characteristics was not investigated. The low-cost air-quality case is
different in kind, because the enclosure contains watts of continuously
operating electronics: Couzo et al. (2024) co-located a PurpleAir unit against
research-grade probes for 553 days (148,230 paired records) and measured a
temperature bias of +2.6 degC and a relative-humidity bias of -17.4 percent,
with the bias growing from 1.8 degC in cold air to 4.2 degC above 25 degC, and
Barkjohn et al. (2021) showed that this onboard temperature and humidity signal
is what a nationwide particulate-matter correction is built on, so the enclosure's
thermal error propagates directly into reported pollutant concentrations.

(Peer-reviewed articles cited: Nakamura and Mahrt 2005; Tarara and Hoheisel
2007; Holden et al. 2013; Theisen et al. 2020; Botero-Valencia et al. 2022;
Bernard et al. 2019; Couzo et al. 2024; Barkjohn et al. 2021; Deford et al.
2025. Full bibliographic entries: paper/references.bib.)

====================================================================
3. RESEARCH QUESTION
====================================================================

GAP (one sentence):
Existing work either ranks shield geometries empirically or corrects an
enclosure's thermal error after the enclosure has been built - by fitting
coefficients or a regression against a reference instrument - so no available
model predicts the temperature and humidity bias of a low-cost 3D-printed
air-quality enclosure before it is fabricated, and the two mechanisms that
distinguish these enclosures from meteorological screens, the heat dissipated by
their own electronics and conduction through a low-conductivity printed wall, are
absent from the models currently in use.

QUESTION:
Can the temperature and relative-humidity bias of a 3D-printed low-cost
air-quality sensor enclosure be predicted before fabrication from its design
variables - surface absorptance, vent geometry, wall thickness and conductivity,
and internal electronics power - and in which operating regimes does that
prediction break down?

Narrower fallback if the pre-build framing is judged too ambitious for the scope
available: "How do surface finish and ventilation geometry affect the temperature
and relative-humidity bias of this project's outdoor sensor enclosure under
different solar-radiation and wind conditions?" This is an honest
design/ranking study and is achievable with three variants and one reference.

====================================================================
4. EVIDENCE BOUNDARY (state this if asked for numbers)
====================================================================

The project currently holds a literature synthesis and a first-order analytical
thermal model. At 1000 W/m2 solar irradiance and 0.5 m/s wind that model predicts
a sensor temperature rise above true ambient of 19.4 degC for the dark baseline
box, 4.5 degC for the same geometry with a white finish, and 3.0 degC for a
passive multiplate shield. These are MODEL PREDICTIONS, not measurements; no
co-location data for this enclosure exists yet. Two further honest points:

- A companion analysis found that combining plausible assumption changes can
  reverse the predicted advantage of the shield over the painted box, so the
  ranking above is not robust and should not be presented as a result.
- A dimensionless reduction of the same model predicts its own solar-driven
  regime to about 0.1 degC but fails by several degC once the predicted bias
  exceeds roughly 20 degC, so any prediction claim must state the regime it
  applies to.

Calibration, co-location against a reference instrument, and thermal validation
remain future work and require PI approval and equipment access.
